**Use this when** you are about to email, call, or walk into a room with a
person or company, and you have one page of attention to spend on them.

## Identity first, always

Common names merge. Two people with the same name become one brief, and then you
open with a reference to the wrong person's company and the call is over before
it starts.

So before anything else I pin them to one identity using two facts that match:
employer plus city, employer plus title, or a company name plus its registration
state. If I cannot get two, every line below is labeled `GUESSED` and the brief
says at the top that the subject is unconfirmed. This check is not optional and
it is not a formality. It is the single most expensive thing to get wrong.

## What goes on the page

1. **Who they are in their own words.** Quote the About page or the profile
   headline. Not a paraphrase, the actual line.
2. **What they sell, and to whom.**
3. **Size signals that are checkable.** Team page headcount, number of
   locations, registration date, open roles. Never an employee count I inferred.
4. **Something that changed in the last 90 days.** A new location, a new hire,
   a price change, an award, a filing, a job posting. This is where the opening
   line comes from.
5. **The hook.** One sentence that only works on this prospect. If it would work
   on any company in the category, it is not a hook and I say so.
6. **The one thing I do not know that would change the pitch.** Named, with the
   question that would settle it.

Six items. Anything else is padding that pushes the hook off the page.

## Where it comes from

Their own site first: About, Team, Careers, News, and the footer, which carries
the legal entity name and sometimes the registration number.

Then, as needed:

- The state business registry. Search "<state> secretary of state business
  search". Gives entity status, registration date, and the registered agent.
  Status is the useful one. An entity in bad standing is a different
  conversation.
- SEC EDGAR full text search, https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/, which covers
  the full text of electronic filings since 2001. Useful for public companies
  and for anyone named inside somebody else's filing.
- Public reviews, for what customers complain about in their own words.
- For trades and construction, the local permit or licence lookup, which shows
  real recent activity rather than claimed activity.

## The boundary

Public business facts only. No home address, no personal phone number, no
family, nothing behind a login, and no assembling a profile out of scattered
personal details. The test: if reading the line aloud on the call would make
them uncomfortable, it does not go on the page.

## Limits

**The trap.** Identity collision, and the naive version never checks for it. It
confidently merges two companies with similar names in the same state, or a
person with their namesake, and every line after that is wrong in a way that
sounds informed. The second trap is a hook built on a detail invented to fit,
which the prospect recognizes as not quite them.

A brief tells you what is true about them, not whether they will buy. It cannot
see revenue, headcount, their current vendor, or their budget, and it will not
estimate any of those.

It goes stale in weeks, and it is worth nothing if you read it aloud instead of
asking.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
